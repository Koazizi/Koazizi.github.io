Join the lab

Research urban water systems where physical and human processes meet

The UHR Lab is building its first student cohort at Tennessee State University. Students join a coherent research program focused on why water and climate-resilience interventions fall short of their technical promise, and how to improve the performance they deliver.

Research fit

A strong fit begins with the scientific question

The lab is a good home for students who want to connect hydrology and infrastructure to behavior, institutions, spatial inequality and real decisions. Projects may be computational, empirical or community-engaged, but they should explain a mechanism rather than only produce a map or run a model.

The question connecting the lab

When does a technically promising intervention become reliable and equitable system performance, and what prevents that transition?

Where students can contribute

Where things stand

The lab opened in January 2026 and is building its first cohort. Early students will help establish data systems, computational workflows, partnerships and lab culture while developing research that is clearly their own.

Funding. Graduate support can come from departmental teaching assistantships, research assistantships and externally funded projects. Availability depends on the start term and active awards. Please state whether funding is required in your first email; I will be direct about what is available before you invest in a formal application.

Who I am looking for

Ph.D. students

Students who want to develop an independent research line within the lab's broader program, combine methods thoughtfully, publish first-author work and present at major conferences.

M.S. students

Students interested in a focused thesis on a defined part of an active project, such as a hydrologic model, spatial diagnostic, network analysis or empirical case study.

Undergraduate researchers

TSU students interested in GIS, environmental data, hydrology, literature synthesis, visualization or clearly bounded research questions connected to a course or lab project.

Collaborators & visiting researchers

Researchers, agencies, utilities and community organizations working on urban water, flooding, infrastructure resilience or environmental decision-making.

Training

What students learn to do

The lab is interdisciplinary, but technical depth matters. Training is organized around the ability to connect a mechanism, a method and a decision context.

Physical systems

Model water and infrastructure

Hydrologic and hydrodynamic modeling, rainfall-runoff analysis, infrastructure networks and uncertainty.

Spatial evidence

Build reproducible geospatial analysis

GIS, remote sensing, parcel and neighborhood analysis, spatial statistics and documented data workflows.

Coupled dynamics

Represent behavior and feedbacks

Agent-based models, system dynamics, scenario analysis and interpretable computational experiments.

Institutions & communities

Study decisions and networks

Surveys, interviews, network analysis, participatory methods and careful treatment of community knowledge.

Backgrounds that fit

Civil and environmental engineering is a common route into the lab, but not the only one. Hydrology, geography and GIS, data science, statistics, planning, public policy, sustainability and environmental social science can all fit when the proposed work connects clearly to the research program.

Helpful preparation includes some programming in Python or R, GIS, statistics, technical writing, or experience with a model such as SWMM or HEC-RAS. You do not need all of these before joining. Evidence that you can learn independently, reason carefully and communicate clearly matters more than a long software list.

How to contact me

Write to kazizi@tnstate.edu. A concise message is better than a generic cover letter. Please attach a CV and include:

  1. Your intended start term and degree.
  2. The research front or question that interests you, and why it fits your goals.
  3. One paper or project from the lab that you read, plus a question, limitation or next step you noticed.
  4. Your relevant preparation, including research, coursework, programming, GIS, modeling, fieldwork or professional experience.
  5. Whether you require funding.

A strong initial email does not need to present a finished dissertation topic. It should show that you understand the lab's direction and can identify a plausible place to contribute.

How I advise

Research ownership
Students should develop intellectual ownership of the work they lead. If you lead the study, the default expectation is first authorship.
Meetings
Graduate students meet regularly one-to-one, with group meetings added as the lab grows. Meetings are for clarifying decisions and obstacles, not only reporting progress.
Writing
Writing begins early. Students receive detailed feedback because research questions, analysis and prose become clearer together.
Reproducibility
Code, data decisions and model assumptions should be documented well enough for another researcher to inspect and reproduce the analytical path.
Community-facing research
Residents, agencies and utility practitioners are treated as partners with expertise. Consent and data restrictions are respected even when they limit what can be released.
Career development
Training is adapted to the student's goals in academia, public agencies, consulting or industry, while maintaining strong research and communication standards.
Sustainable work
Research is demanding, but chronic overwork is not a substitute for good planning. Students should raise workload and resource problems before they become crises.
