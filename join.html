Joining the lab

This page is longer than most “contact me” pages on purpose. Choosing an advisor is a multi-year decision, and you should be able to judge the fit before you write to me — not after.

Where things stand

The lab opened at Tennessee State University in January 2026 and is building its first cohort. That is a real advantage if you want it: early students help set the direction, get more of my time than they would in an established group, and are first authors on the work they lead. It also means you will be joining something that is still being built, which suits some people and not others.

Funding. Graduate support in the lab comes from a mix of departmental teaching and research assistantships and externally funded projects — currently including a University Transportation Center award on roadway flooding in Nashville. What is available depends on the start term, so ask me directly what the position looks like for the term you are aiming at, and I will tell you plainly rather than leave you guessing.

Who I am looking for

Ph.D. students

The core of the lab. You would take ownership of one research line — roadway flooding and emergency access, green stormwater infrastructure, flood-insurance protection gaps, or governance networks — and develop it into a body of work that is yours.

M.S. students

Thesis work on a well-defined piece of a live project: a modelling study, a spatial analysis, or an empirical study of a specific system. A good route into a Ph.D., and a good route into practice.

Undergraduate researchers

TSU undergraduates are welcome, including students in my hydrology and environmental engineering courses. Typical projects involve GIS mapping, data preparation, visualisation or literature synthesis, and they are real contributions — several of the students I have advised became co-authors.

Visiting researchers & collaborators

I am glad to hear from agency staff, utility engineers, planners and community organisations working on flooding or stormwater in Middle Tennessee, and from researchers wanting to collaborate on proposals.

What background helps

Civil and environmental engineering is the most common route in, but it is not the only one. Geography and GIS, data science, statistics, urban planning, hydrology, and the environmental social sciences all fit — the lab works across that boundary by design, and the strongest projects usually sit on it.

Useful before you arrive: some programming (Python or R), some GIS, and comfort with statistics. Genuinely useful but rarer: experience with interviews or survey design, or with a hydrologic model such as SWMM or HEC-RAS.

What I care about more than any of that is whether you can hold a question steady for long enough to answer it properly, write clearly, and say “I don’t know yet” without flinching. Those are harder to teach than software.

How to get in touch

Five steps. The first two are the ones people skip, and they matter most.

Step 01

Read two papers

Pick two from the publications page that interest you. If none of them do, this is probably not the right lab — and that is useful to know now.

Step 02

Work out the fit

Look at the research page and identify which theme your interests sit in. Being specific here is what separates a strong enquiry from a mass email.

Step 03

Email me

Write to kazizi@tnstate.edu with the details below. Put the term you want to start in the subject line.

Step 04

We talk

If there is a plausible fit, we set up a video call — a conversation about research and about how you like to work, not an interrogation.

Step 05

Apply formally

Admission is decided by the university, not by me alone. I will tell you what I can support before you spend money on an application.

What to put in the email

Short is fine. Four things, and please attach a CV:

  1. Which of my papers you read, and one thing you would do differently or next. This single sentence tells me more than a page of general enthusiasm.
  2. What you want to work on — a question, a system, or a method. It does not have to be fully formed.
  3. What you have done — degree, relevant coursework, software you have actually used, any research or practice experience.
  4. When you want to start, and whether you need funding to make it possible.

You do not need an introduction from someone I know, and a long formal cover letter does not help your case. If I have not replied within two weeks, please send a short reminder — email gets lost, and a nudge is not an imposition.

How I advise

You are entitled to know this before you commit several years to it, so here it is plainly.

Meetings
Weekly one-to-one with each graduate student, plus a group meeting once the lab is large enough to need one. If a week is unproductive, the meeting still happens — that is usually when it is most useful.
Authorship
If you lead the work, you are first author. I discuss authorship at the start of a project rather than at the end, and undergraduate contributions count.
Writing
You will write a lot, and you will get detailed edits back. That is training, not criticism. Clear writing and clear thinking are the same skill.
Conferences
I expect students to present their work — AGU and ASCE congresses are where this field talks to itself — and I will support the travel where funding allows.
Hours
Research is demanding, but sustained overwork produces bad science. Take your holidays, and tell me if the load is wrong rather than absorbing it quietly.
Community-facing work
Some projects involve residents, agencies and utilities directly. If you work on those, you will be expected to treat the people involved as partners with expertise, not as data sources.
Where you go next
Academia, agency, consultancy or industry — all legitimate. Tell me which you are aiming at and I will shape the training toward it.

Ready

Get in touch.

Email kazizi@tnstate.edu with the four things above. Applications to the graduate programme go through Tennessee State University; write to me before you apply so we can work out whether it is worth it.
