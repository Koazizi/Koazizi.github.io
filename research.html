Research

Three themes, one coupled system

The lab works on urban water under climate stress. The three themes below differ in what they measure — water, infrastructure, or institutions — but they share one premise: outcomes are produced by the interaction, so studying any one alone gives the wrong answer.

Theme 01

Climate risk & urban flood dynamics

Where does floodwater actually go in a city, and who carries the cost when it gets there?

Design storms and regulatory floodplain maps describe a city that does not quite exist. Real urban flooding is shaped by undersized and ageing drainage, pavement, local topography that no regional model resolves, and the sequence of storms a neighbourhood happens to receive. We combine hydrologic and hydrodynamic modelling with spatial risk analytics and observations from residents to characterise pluvial flooding at the scale where people experience it.

The second half of the theme is burden. Physical exposure is only the first step toward a loss: insurance take-up, deductibles, public assistance and household finances all sit between the water and the outcome. We are mapping where those protection gaps concentrate, and why they differ by hazard type and location.

  • Pluvial flood modelling
  • SWMM · HEC-RAS · IBER
  • Flood exposure mapping
  • Flood insurance
  • Transportation disruption

Projects

Transportation network vulnerability to roadway flooding in Nashville

Principal Investigator · Tennessee State University · CHDT University Transportation Center · 2026–2027

A coupled hazard–network–behaviour framework for emergency access: how flood hazard, road network structure, traveller response and emergency-response institutions jointly determine whether people can still reach hospitals, shelters and work when streets go under.

Flood insurance protection gaps across the United States

Lead Investigator · 2025–present

A national analysis of how physical flood risk, insurance institutions and public assistance interact to leave geographically distinct protection gaps. Early results suggest the gap looks different depending on whether the driving hazard is riverine, coastal or pluvial. Presented at AGU26; manuscript in preparation.

Community-based and citizen-science urban flood research

Lead Researcher · The University of Memphis · 2018–2022

Structured resident observations, field data and 1D–2D modelling combined to characterise urban pluvial flooding in North Memphis — and to test whether local knowledge measurably improves model performance rather than merely supplementing it.

Theme 02

Sustainable urban water & green infrastructure

Distributed stormwater infrastructure only works if households actually build and keep it. What decides whether they do?

Cities increasingly rely on green stormwater infrastructure installed on private land: rain gardens, cisterns, permeable surfaces. That makes the performance of a public drainage strategy depend on thousands of private decisions, each shaped by cost, parcel feasibility, awareness, social influence and whether a neighbour did it first. We model those decisions explicitly — with surveys, structural equation modelling and agent-based simulation — and then ask what the resulting spatial pattern of adoption means for runoff and for equity.

The same logic applies to drinking water. Conservation programmes, rate structures and affordability targets interact through utility revenue in ways that are easy to get wrong. We use system dynamics and comparative analysis of U.S. utilities to identify the conditions under which cities sustain water that is both high quality and affordable.

  • Agent-based modelling
  • Structural equation modelling
  • System dynamics
  • Water affordability
  • Conservation policy

Projects

Coupled human–infrastructure dynamics of green stormwater infrastructure

Lead Investigator · Tennessee State University and The University of Texas at Austin · 2023–present

How household behaviour, social influence, drainage connectivity and maintenance institutions jointly determine the reliability and equity of distributed stormwater protection — that is, the difference between installations counted and protection actually delivered.

Transition dynamics in integrated urban drinking water systems

Researcher · Arizona State University · 2022–2024

System dynamics modelling, comparative utility analysis and practitioner engagement, examining how infrastructure, institutions and conservation behaviour shape water system reliability, affordability and adaptation — including a detailed study of the Miami-Dade Water and Sewer Department.

Theme 03

Community, governance & decision support

Flood risk is managed by dozens of organisations that were never designed to work together. Where does the coordination actually fail?

No single agency owns urban flood risk. Drainage districts, city departments, councils of government, state agencies, federal programmes, utilities and non-profits each hold part of it, and the seams between them are where preparation and response break down. We use interviews, surveys and network analysis — including exponential random graph models — to measure how those organisations actually connect, rather than how an organisational chart says they should.

Alongside that runs a commitment to knowledge that models do not contain. Residents know which intersection floods first and which storm drain has been blocked for years. Citizen science, participatory mapping and fuzzy cognitive mapping let us bring that into analysis in a form that is auditable, and return results in a form that planners can use.

  • Social network analysis
  • ERGM
  • Stakeholder interviews
  • Fuzzy cognitive mapping
  • Citizen science
  • Decision support

Projects

Collaborative flood governance on the U.S. Gulf Coast

Lead Investigator · The University of Texas at Austin · 2024–2026

Stakeholder interviews, surveys and network analysis examining how institutional relationships and coordination shape the reliability and equity of regional flood-risk governance — and where multi-sector perspectives on the same flood diverge.

Project CRESSLE — community resilience in an Earth-system science learning ecosystem

Researcher · The University of Texas at Austin · 2024–present

Integrating atmospheric, land-cover, geospatial and social-vulnerability data to examine how environmental hazards and community conditions combine to produce unequal exposure and resilience to extreme heat and precipitation.
